# WormWise Fall Armyworm Offline Reference

## 1. Purpose and authority

This document is the governed content baseline for WormWise's offline Advisor, assessment explanations, evidence labels, safety messages, and future AI grounding. It is written for maize assessment in the Philippines. It is not a pesticide label, diagnosis from a laboratory, treatment prescription, or replacement for the Department of Agriculture (DA), Bureau of Plant Industry (BPI), Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority (FPA), Regional Crop Protection Centers (RCPCs), local government agriculture offices, or qualified crop-protection professionals.

The reference separates four kinds of content:

- **Verified core** — supported by Philippine government material or broadly applicable FAO, IPPC, or CABI guidance.
- **Operational app rule** — a WormWise workflow decision that must remain consistent with the app's assessment protocol.
- **Expert validation required** — plausible content that cannot be converted into Philippine field advice without review by a Philippine crop-protection specialist.
- **Prohibited content** — material the app must not display as actionable advice.

All downstream guide cards, search results, AI prompts, and generated answers should preserve these distinctions.

## 2. Mandatory editorial and safety restrictions

### 2.1 Source hierarchy

Use sources in this order:

1. Current Philippine law, FPA registration data, pesticide labels, and DA/BPI national protocols.
2. Current DA regional advisories and RCPC materials for regional reporting or locally observed conditions.
3. FAO and International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC) standards and field guidance.
4. CABI identification and integrated-pest-management references.
5. Peer-reviewed studies, clearly labeled by country, crop, environment, and study design.
6. Manufacturer material only for a registered product's approved label; never as independent proof of efficacy.

Blogs, retailer pages, social posts, generative-AI output, unattributed images, and unsourced farmer remedies are not authoritative sources.

### 2.2 Content that must not be included as advice

WormWise must not:

- Recommend an insecticide product, active ingredient, application rate, mixture, interval, or application method unless it is currently registered by the Philippine FPA for the exact crop and pest and the statement reproduces the current approved label accurately.
- infer that registration for drone application means registration for every application method, or vice versa;
- retain expired registration information as an actionable recommendation;
- tell a farmer to mix pesticides, exceed a label rate, shorten a re-entry interval, ignore a pre-harvest interval, spray without required personal protective equipment, or reuse pesticide containers;
- provide a universal treatment threshold when Philippine authorities have not validated it for the relevant maize growth stage, location, production system, and assessment method;
- interpret pheromone-trap capture alone as proof that a field is infested or needs spraying;
- identify FAW from leaf damage, frass, an egg mass, or a poor-quality image alone;
- claim that an AI or image classifier has confirmed a pest diagnosis;
- recommend introducing, culturing, releasing, or spreading a biological control organism or pathogen without applicable Philippine authorization and expert supervision;
- recommend homemade pathogen recycling, ash, sand, soil, lime, salt, soap, oil, botanical extracts, or similar remedies as proven safe and effective Philippine treatments. Some international guides describe farmer experimentation with such practices, but that does not establish Philippine efficacy, crop safety, worker safety, legality, or environmental safety.^1
- recommend destroying a crop, imposing quarantine, or moving infested plant material without direction from the proper Philippine authority;
- imply that visible injury always causes economic yield loss or that absence of a visible larva proves absence of FAW;
- hide uncertainty or omit the date, jurisdiction, and source of a management recommendation.

### 2.3 Required warnings

Any guide mentioning pesticides must say: **Use only an FPA-registered product for maize and fall armyworm, follow the complete current label, wear the required protective equipment, and observe re-entry and pre-harvest intervals. Consult the Municipal/City Agriculture Office or RCPC when uncertain.** FAO warns that pesticide use can create human-health and environmental risks, harm natural enemies, and select for resistance.^2

Any identification guide must say: **A photograph or damage symptom can support screening but may not confirm species. When identification affects control decisions, collect evidence safely and request confirmation from an agriculture officer or crop-protection specialist.**

Any threshold or management-decision guide must say: **Do not make a pesticide decision from a risk label alone. Consider current field scouting, crop stage, fresh evidence, larval size, crop condition, natural enemies, local guidance, product cost, and label restrictions.**

## 3. Scope

The verified scope is FAW (*Spodoptera frugiperda*) in maize. FAW can use many host plants, and Philippine research has documented concern beyond maize, including rice, but the existing Philippine corn protocol should not automatically be applied to rice or other crops.^3 WormWise should label non-maize content as outside the current assessment protocol.

The first officially reported Philippine corn damage was in Piat, Cagayan, in June 2019.^4 The DA subsequently established surveillance, reporting, technical-assistance, and intervention responsibilities involving its National and Regional Corn Programs, Regional Crop Pest Management Centers, and LGUs.^5

### 3.1 DA-BPI printed fall armyworm leaflet

The DA Bureau of Plant Industry Crop Pest Management Division's printed leaflet, *Detection and Management of Fall Armyworm*, was supplied as six photographed pages. The visible management panel lists crop diversification or rotation, synchronous planting, quality seed, field sanitation, surveys, pheromone traps, physical removal of egg masses and larvae, biological control, and chemical control. It directs suspected FAW reporting and validation to crop-pest offices. The photographed pages do not establish a verified publication date, edition, public URL, current pesticide registration, or a locally validated economic threshold. WormWise therefore cites it for general Philippine prevention and monitoring context only. Do not turn its biological or chemical categories into product, organism-release, dose, or field-specific treatment instructions without current qualified review.

### 3.2 Evaluation of the CIMMYT Asia IPM guide

The 2021 CIMMYT publication *Fall Armyworm in Asia: A Guide for Integrated Pest Management* is suitable as a major regional technical reference for WormWise. It was edited by B. M. Prasanna, J. E. Huesing, V. M. Peschke, and R. Eddy and developed for plant-protection, research, extension, government, and seed-system professionals serving Asian smallholders.^15 It improves this reference in five areas:

- an Asia-specific IPM framework rather than direct reliance on African farming-system examples;
- formal separation of surveillance, simplified scouting, formal scouting, monitoring, and action thresholds;
- a decision framework combining pest pressure, maize stage, environment/weather, and intervention feasibility;
- explicit evaluation of interventions by cost, efficacy, safety, accessibility, and scalability (CESAS);
- communication and content-governance guidance, including collaborative technical review, local-language adaptation, and updates as evidence changes.

The guide is **not Philippine regulation or a Philippine pesticide label**. Its numerical action thresholds are presented for Asian adaptation and must remain non-actionable in WormWise until Philippine authorities validate them against the app's sampling definitions. Its pesticide hazard tables cannot establish current FPA registration. Its country case studies, laboratory rearing methods, parasitoid-release methods, refuge designs, and specific agronomic systems must not be converted directly into farmer instructions.

Copyright also matters. CIMMYT permits downloading, printing, storing, sharing, and fair use with attribution, but its repository notice asks users to contact the CIMMYT Knowledge Center before distributing a translation or other derivative work.^15 WormWise may cite and summarize the guide; publication of adapted diagrams, extensive extracts, or translated derivative content requires a rights review.

## 4. Identification

### 4.1 What FAW is

FAW is the larval stage of a noctuid moth. Its complete life cycle has four broad stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult moth. The larval period contains six instars. Development time varies with temperature and other environmental conditions, so day counts are approximate rather than a field diagnosis.^1

### 4.2 Egg masses

Verified identification cues:

- Eggs are laid in clusters, often around 100–200 in a mass in the CABI handbook.
- Masses are commonly placed on maize leaves and may be covered with pale or grayish scales from the female moth.
- Eggs can occur on other plant surfaces, particularly when populations are high.
- A single image of an egg mass is not definitive because other moths also lay clustered eggs.

Offline action: show photographs of uncovered, partly covered, and heavily scaled egg masses. Ask the user to photograph the whole mass and nearby leaf, without touching pesticide residues or unknown organisms.

### 4.3 Larvae

The most useful combination of mature-larva features is:

- a pale, inverted Y-shaped marking on a darker head capsule;
- four prominent dark spots arranged approximately as a square on the second-to-last abdominal segment;
- raised dark spots elsewhere on the body, often with longitudinal stripes;
- color that can vary substantially from greenish to brown or nearly black.

No single feature is sufficient. Small larvae may not show mature markings clearly, and some related caterpillars can show a Y-like head marking. The CABI handbook explicitly advises comparing several characters.^1

Large larvae are often found singly or in very small numbers in a whorl because older larvae are cannibalistic. Finding only one large caterpillar does not indicate low earlier pressure.

### 4.4 Pupa

FAW normally pupates in soil, often a few centimeters below the surface, in a loose oval earthen or silken chamber. Pupae are reddish brown and difficult to distinguish from related noctuid moth pupae in the field.^1 Do not encourage routine digging that damages roots merely to confirm an app diagnosis.

### 4.5 Adult moth

Adult males and females differ in wing pattern, and field recognition is difficult. A pheromone trap is a monitoring aid, not a general-purpose species-confirmation tool. Lure specificity, trap design, placement, maintenance, and expert examination affect interpretation.

## 5. Look-alikes and false attribution

WormWise should teach a process of exclusion instead of presenting a single “FAW or not” image comparison.

### 5.1 Caterpillar look-alikes

Potential look-alikes relevant to a Philippine maize guide include other *Spodoptera* species, corn earworm (*Helicoverpa armigera*), true armyworm, grass armyworm, common cutworm, and maize stem borers such as Asian corn borer. The exact local comparison set and photographs require review by BPI/RCPC entomologists before publication.

Comparison fields required for every species:

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Head | markings, color, and whether an inverted-Y-like mark may occur |
| Rear abdomen | arrangement and prominence of spots |
| Body | hairs, tubercles, stripes, texture, and color range |
| Plant location | exposed leaf, whorl, stalk, tassel, silk, or ear |
| Damage | surface feeding, holes, tunneling, cutting, ear feeding |
| Behavior | hiding, curling, boring, movement, and typical abundance |
| Confidence | features that support identification and features that remain ambiguous |

CABI notes that cotton bollworm can show a broadly similar dot pattern and a Y-like head marking, while windowpane injury can be confused with stem-borer injury.^1 Therefore, damage alone should be labeled **possible FAW evidence**, not confirmed FAW.

### 5.2 Non-insect look-alikes

The offline library must distinguish pest evidence from:

- hail, wind, animal, equipment, or handling damage;
- old healed feeding injury;
- nutrient deficiency or physiological discoloration;
- herbicide or fertilizer injury;
- disease lesions, mold, and ear rots;
- soil or plant debris lodged in the whorl;
- drought, waterlogging, and poor emergence.

These comparisons require original or licensed Philippine field images reviewed by an agronomist or plant pathologist. They should not be generated synthetically for diagnostic training.

## 6. Life cycle and field ecology

Eggs may hatch in roughly three to five days under conditions described in the CABI handbook. Larvae may mature in roughly 14–22 days, then drop to the ground to pupate; adult emergence follows after the pupal period. These are indicative ranges and must not be used to calculate an exact infestation date.^1

Young larvae feed superficially and can disperse on silk carried by wind. Older larvae move into protected plant structures, especially the whorl. On reproductive maize, larvae may move toward the tassel, silk, husk, and ear. Adult moth movement can be extensive, so an individual field cannot be treated as an isolated system.

Heavy rain may reduce exposed eggs and young larvae, but rain does not guarantee control. Weather should influence a recommendation to reinspect, not automatically change an infestation result.

## 7. Symptoms and evidence

### 7.1 Evidence classes

WormWise should use the following conceptual hierarchy:

1. **Current direct evidence** — a larva with multiple FAW-compatible features, a diagnostically reviewed specimen, or another locally accepted direct sign.
2. **Current supporting evidence** — fresh feeding, fresh frass, or a recent egg mass compatible with FAW.
3. **Historical or ambiguous evidence** — old holes, healed injury, dry debris, unclear images, or damage without current activity.
4. **Contradictory evidence** — characteristics more consistent with another pest, disease, or physical injury.

The app may compute an assessment classification from its protocol, but the guide must explain what was observed rather than claim laboratory certainty.

### 7.2 Leaf evidence

- **Pinhole/windowpane injury:** small larvae scrape leaf tissue, leaving translucent areas or small holes after the leaf expands.
- **Ragged whorl injury:** larger larvae create irregular elongated holes that align across an unfurled leaf.
- **Freshness:** moist, bright, newly disrupted tissue and current frass support recent activity; dry, browned edges and damage confined to older leaves should not be counted as current infestation without additional evidence.

FAO's scouting note specifically says not to count plants that show only older-leaf damage with no clear current damage.^6

### 7.3 Frass

Frass is larval excrement. Larger feeding larvae can produce coarse, moist clumps that later become dry and sawdust-like.^1 Frass supports active internal or protected feeding but is not species-specific. WormWise must not identify FAW solely from frass.

### 7.4 Tassel, silk, and ear evidence

Older maize can show feeding around emerging tassels, silks, husks, ear tips, entry points, and kernels. Ear injury can increase the opportunity for fungal contamination and reduce grain quality.^1 Because ear damage has several insect and disease causes, reproductive-stage identification must examine multiple zones and avoid attributing mold or rot to FAW without evidence.

## 8. Growth-stage inspection guide

The following is an app-oriented inspection map, not a universal treatment table.

| Maize stage | Primary inspection emphasis | Supporting observations | Important cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| VE–V2 | stand establishment, whole seedling, missing or cut plants | egg masses, small larvae, fresh surface feeding | distinguish non-emergence, drought, disease, and cutworm injury |
| V3–V6 | newest leaves and whorl | windowpaning, pinholes, fresh frass, larva | old expanded-leaf holes can exaggerate current activity |
| V7–V12 | newest 3–4 leaves, deeper whorl, emerging tassel when present | ragged injury, frass, larval size, natural enemies | do not assume dramatic leaf appearance equals proportional yield loss |
| VT–R2 | tassel, ear base, silk, upper and lower leaf axils | fresh feeding, frass, larva, husk entry | inspect safely and avoid damaging pollination structures |
| R3–R6 | exterior ear tip, silk/husk, base/shank/axils; opened-ear subsample only if protocol requires | entry hole, frass freshness, larva, kernel feeding, mold/rot-like symptoms | distinguish external evidence from destructive confirmation; observe mycotoxin and food-safety precautions |

CABI recommends inspecting the newest two or three leaves emerging from the funnel in younger plants and the newest three or four leaves plus the emerging tassel later.^1 WormWise's exact stage workflow must remain synchronized with its formally approved protocol.

## 9. Scouting protocol

### 9.1 When to scout

Monitoring should begin soon after emergence because eggs and small larvae are easier to detect and manage before larvae become protected deep in the whorl. CABI recommends beginning about one week after planting and visiting frequently; its handbook describes walking fields every three to four days while conducting a formal check at least weekly.^1 FAO says at least weekly and more often when conditions are changing rapidly.^6

WormWise should distinguish:

- **routine field visit:** rapid observation of crop health and new activity;
- **formal assessment:** the app's fixed, reproducible station-and-plant protocol;
- **follow-up assessment:** a new formal assessment after the locally appropriate interval, not edits to make an earlier result look current.

### 9.2 Field definition

Sample areas separately when they differ materially in planting date, variety, crop stage, intercropping, irrigation, fertility, or other management. FAO explicitly advises sampling such plots separately.^6 Roads, houses, ponds, wooded areas, and non-maize land must not be included in the assessment polygon or treated as plantable maize area.

If an uploaded boundary is too large for the WormWise block protocol, divide it only along agronomically meaningful and accessible divisions. Do not create a geometric split that combines dissimilar crop stages or directs users across hazards.

### 9.3 Station-and-plant sampling

The international reference method selects five locations and inspects ten consecutive plants at each location, for 50 plants total. At each station, inspect all ten—including unaffected plants—and record zero observations. Excluding clean plants inflates estimated prevalence.^1

The route should cover the cultivated field rather than following only edges or obvious damage. The generated W or ladder path is guidance; users should detour around roads, water, steep ground, pesticide-treated areas, machinery, animals, and other hazards. A detour should not move the station deliberately toward visible damage.

### 9.4 Counting current evidence

At each plant:

- inspect the stage-appropriate plant zones;
- distinguish current from old damage;
- record presence and absence explicitly;
- record uncertainty rather than forcing a positive identification;
- count one plant once even when it has multiple signs;
- keep egg-mass and larval counts separate from the number of affected plants;
- preserve evidence association with the correct field, block, station, plant, date, and evaluator.

For data definitions, WormWise should keep separate fields for percentage of plants with small fresh windowpanes, percentage with an infested whorl, percentage meeting the app's current-infestation definition, larval size class, and moth counts. CIMMYT treats these as distinct monitoring measures; combining them would make a threshold or trend uninterpretable.^15

### 9.5 Pesticide re-entry

Do not scout immediately after pesticide application. Follow the product-label re-entry interval; if the product, application time, or interval is unknown, do not enter until a responsible person confirms safe re-entry.^1

## 10. Pheromone traps

Pheromone traps attract male moths and can support early warning and population monitoring. They do not measure larval injury directly and do not establish a treatment need. A trap catch should trigger timely field scouting for egg masses, larvae, and fresh damage—not automatic spraying. FAO reiterated this distinction in recent biological-control work.^7

Only locally approved trap and lure instructions should become operational guidance. Trap height, lure replacement, kill-strip handling, servicing frequency, and disposal can vary by product. Any insecticidal strip must be treated as hazardous and handled according to its label.

## 11. Interpreting assessment results

### 11.1 What a percentage means

For a fixed sample of 50 plants:

`percent of plants meeting criterion = qualifying plants / 50 × 100`

The criterion must be named. “Fresh damage,” “FAW-compatible evidence,” “visible larva,” and “egg mass” are different measures and must not be merged into an unlabeled infestation percentage.

### 11.2 What a result does not mean

- It is not an exact census of every plant.
- It does not prove that every injury was caused by FAW.
- It does not directly predict yield loss.
- It is not, by itself, an instruction to apply pesticide.
- It does not remain current indefinitely.

Maize can compensate for some leaf injury, and visually severe damage does not always translate proportionally into yield loss.^1 Any economic recommendation must consider crop stage and field context.

### 11.3 Threshold restrictions

International sources publish different action thresholds, sometimes based on damage, egg masses, crop age, or local economics. Those values are not automatically transferable to the Philippines. WormWise may explain the concept of a threshold but must not publish a numeric treatment trigger as official Philippine advice until BPI/RCPC and a qualified Philippine maize specialist approve:

- the exact observation definition;
- sample design and denominator;
- maize growth stages;
- geographic and production context;
- economic assumptions;
- permitted management options;
- review and expiration date.

Until then, WormWise risk levels should direct users to reassessment or expert consultation, not prescribe chemical control.

### 11.4 Risk assessment and follow-up logic

CIMMYT describes field decisions as an interaction among pest pressure, maize growth stage, and environmental conditions—not a lookup based on damage percentage alone.^15 WormWise should therefore show the evidence behind a risk result and keep these contextual fields separate:

- current evidence measure and denominator;
- predominant larval size, when observed reliably;
- crop growth stage and crop condition;
- recent or forecast heavy rain;
- natural-enemy observations;
- recent intervention and safe re-entry time;
- uncertainty, inaccessible stations, and missing evidence.

A no-treatment decision is still an active decision and should produce a follow-up scouting recommendation. CIMMYT suggests shortening the scouting interval after a no-spray decision and rescouting after rain or an intervention.^15 WormWise should adopt the principle, but not hard-code CIMMYT's example intervals until a Philippine protocol owner approves the timing. Weather may justify reassessment; it must not silently change recorded field evidence or claim that rain controlled the infestation.

## 12. Integrated pest management

### 12.1 Decision order

The safe default order is:

1. confirm crop stage and current evidence;
2. assess distribution, natural enemies, weather, and crop condition;
3. use prevention, cultural, mechanical, and biological measures where locally appropriate;
4. reassess;
5. consider a registered pesticide only when justified by current local guidance and the label.

### 12.2 Prevention and crop health

Supported general principles include:

- use good-quality seed appropriate for the area;
- promote vigorous, evenly established maize using appropriate spacing, soil management, fertility, and water management;
- avoid unusually late or staggered maize planting when local production planning permits;
- manage volunteer maize and important alternate grass hosts between crops;
- avoid adjacent sequential plantings that can maintain a continuous susceptible host;
- preserve field diversity and habitat for natural enemies where compatible with Philippine agronomy.

Philippine research found several grass weeds capable of supporting FAW feeding or oviposition and concluded that weed management during crop and fallow periods may help disrupt reinfestation.^4 This does not justify indiscriminate removal of all non-crop vegetation, which may also support beneficial organisms.

### 12.3 Mechanical action

Hand removal or crushing of reachable egg masses and very young larvae is described in FAO and CABI smallholder guidance.^1 This may be practical only at low scale and when it can be done without pesticide exposure, unsafe climbing, skin contact with hazardous residues, or significant plant damage. It is not a substitute for surveillance across the field.

### 12.4 Natural enemies

Predators, parasitoids, and pathogens can cause substantial natural mortality. Broad groups include ants, earwigs, spiders, beetles, predatory bugs, lacewings, social wasps, parasitoid wasps, parasitoid flies, and microbial pathogens. Philippine ATI material mentions earwigs, lacewings, *Trichogramma*, nucleopolyhedrovirus (NPV), and *Metarhizium* in regional education.^8

The offline guide may teach recognition and conservation of naturally occurring beneficial organisms. It must not advise users to rear, transport, release, or formulate them. Species-level claims and release instructions require Philippine validation because natural-enemy communities, approved products, and biosecurity requirements are local.

Conservation practices include minimizing unnecessary broad-spectrum pesticide use, avoiding direct spraying of visible beneficial organisms, and maintaining agronomically appropriate diversity. FAO emphasizes that pesticide overuse can remove natural control and worsen resistance pressure.^9

### 12.5 Biological products

A statement that a bacterium, virus, fungus, parasitoid, or botanical has been studied somewhere is not equivalent to Philippine registration or a label recommendation. Before inclusion, record:

- organism or active ingredient and strain;
- Philippine registration or authorization;
- maize and FAW label claim;
- formulation and storage requirements;
- viable-use conditions and shelf life;
- worker and environmental precautions;
- source and expiry/review date.

### 12.6 Chemical control and resistance

Chemical control is a last, justified component of IPM. Selection should be based on current Philippine registration and label, target life stage, application feasibility, risk to people and beneficial organisms, resistance management, and economics.

Repeated use of the same insecticide mode of action selects for resistance. IRAC recommends rotating effective modes of action across pest generations rather than merely alternating brand names.^10 WormWise should display the IRAC group only when taken from a current label or authoritative database and should never generate a rotation plan without an agronomist reviewing the locally available products.

FPA registration is time-sensitive. The FPA publishes current registered-product information and expiration dates; therefore product content must be stored as an updateable online dataset and must expire offline when its verification period ends.^11

### 12.7 Intervention evaluation using CESAS

Before WormWise presents any management option as locally practical, it should be reviewed using CIMMYT's CESAS criteria:^15

| Criterion | WormWise review question |
|---|---|
| Cost | What are the direct input, labor, equipment, transport, and opportunity costs? Does the recommendation rely on a temporary subsidy? |
| Efficacy | Is there replicated evidence of meaningful crop or yield benefit under comparable conditions, rather than only reduced damage scores or laboratory mortality? |
| Safety | What are the risks to applicators, households, livestock, non-target organisms, natural enemies, water, and harvested grain? |
| Accessibility | Is the option legally available, understandable, usable, and realistically obtainable by the intended Philippine farmer? |
| Scalability | Can the option remain available and effective beyond a trial, donor project, or limited expert-led release? |

Failure on safety or legal availability blocks publication as an actionable recommendation. A technology that performs well in a laboratory or foreign trial should be labeled research evidence, not a farmer recommendation. This applies equally to synthetic pesticides, biopesticides, biological releases, resistant seed, push-pull systems, traps, and digital decision tools.

### 12.8 Host-plant resistance and Bt maize

CIMMYT identifies native genetic resistance and transgenic resistance as potential components of IPM, while emphasizing integration with scouting, cultural practices, biological control, and resistance management.^15 WormWise may explain these concepts but must not label a Philippine variety “FAW resistant” without current Philippine variety, biosafety, seed-registration, and performance evidence. “Tolerant” must not be presented as immune, and planting resistant or Bt maize must not disable scouting.

Any Bt-maize guide requires a Philippine specialist to validate the specific event, target-pest claim, refuge requirement, stewardship instruction, seed availability, and registration status. Foreign refuge designs or performance claims are prohibited as local instructions.

## 13. Worker, household, and environmental safety

### 13.1 Before field entry

- Check whether pesticides were recently applied.
- Identify the product and application time and read the label re-entry interval.
- Do not enter when the interval is unknown, during unsafe weather, or when the route crosses floodwater, unstable ground, active machinery, aggressive animals, or other hazards.
- Wear field-appropriate clothing and carry drinking water and communication suitable for the location.

### 13.2 During pesticide decisions or application

Only trained users should apply pesticides. The full label governs personal protective equipment, mixing, application, buffer zones, re-entry, pre-harvest interval, storage, first aid, disposal, and environmental precautions. WormWise should link to an official label or local officer; it should not compress these requirements into a short generated recipe.

### 13.3 Exposure or poisoning concern

WormWise must not attempt medical diagnosis. Leave the exposure area, avoid further contact, follow the pesticide label's first-aid directions, retain the label/container information without handling a contaminated container unnecessarily, and contact local emergency or poison-control services. Do not induce vomiting unless the label or a medical professional explicitly instructs it.

### 13.4 Grain and ear safety

Ear injury can permit fungal infection and affect grain quality.^1 The app should advise segregating suspicious moldy or badly damaged grain and obtaining current food/feed-safety guidance. It must not declare grain safe for people or animals from an image.

## 14. When to seek expert help or report

Escalate when:

- distinguishing FAW from a look-alike would change management;
- images are unclear or diagnostic characters conflict;
- damage is spreading rapidly or appears outbreak-scale;
- reproductive-stage ear injury, mold, or food/feed safety is involved;
- a pesticide decision is being considered;
- the crop was recently treated but continues to show activity;
- suspected resistance, product failure, poisoning, off-target injury, or environmental harm occurs;
- the crop is not maize or the situation falls outside WormWise's protocol;
- an unusual pest, host, distribution, or symptom is observed.

Philippine reporting should start with the Municipal/City or Provincial Agriculture Office or the relevant DA Regional Crop Protection Center. DA policy assigns LGUs and RCPMCs formal surveillance, incidence-reporting, validation, technical-assistance, and response roles.^5 Contact data should be maintained as a separately updateable directory rather than copied permanently into this reference. DA's regional office directory and BPI plant-pest surveillance directory should be the current lookup sources.^12

When reporting, prepare:

- farmer contact details and permission;
- field, barangay, municipality/city, and province;
- crop, variety if known, planting date, and growth stage;
- affected area and distribution;
- assessment date and method;
- clear photographs of the whole plant, damage, larval head, larval rear, and scale;
- recent pesticide applications and re-entry status;
- a safely collected specimen only when an officer instructs how to collect and transport it.

Every factual claim that could change a field decision must map to at least one source ID. Content without a source may be used only for navigation or plain-language explanation and must not introduce a biological, safety, threshold, or treatment claim.

## 17. Online Advisor grounding rules

The online Advisor should retrieve this reference before answering FAW questions and must:

- cite the underlying source and jurisdiction;
- prefer Philippine guidance over foreign guidance;
- state when Philippine guidance was not found;
- refuse to invent thresholds, pesticide choices, rates, intervals, diagnoses, or biological-release instructions;
- use assessment data only as recorded and never convert missing observations to negative findings;
- distinguish an app risk classification from confirmed pest identification;
- recommend official local help for high-risk or uncertain decisions;
- never claim that being online makes an answer authoritative.

## 18. Content governance and update schedule

| Content class | Review cadence | Required reviewer |
|---|---:|---|
| Identification and biology | every 24 months | entomologist/crop-protection specialist |
| App scouting protocol | every release that changes assessment logic; otherwise annually | protocol owner plus Philippine specialist |
| Thresholds and decision rules | at least annually | BPI/RCPC-aligned maize IPM specialist |
| Pesticide registrations and labels | before display and at least quarterly; expire when unverified | FPA source check plus qualified reviewer |
| Reporting contacts | every 6 months | product/content owner using official directory |
| Safety and emergency wording | annually | pesticide-safety or occupational-health reviewer |
| Images | at onboarding and whenever replaced | entomologist for identity; rights review for licensing |

For new or disputed topics, conduct a content workshop with the protocol owner, Philippine crop-protection reviewer, extension representative, safety reviewer where relevant, and product/content designer. CIMMYT recommends collaborative technical-brief development to prevent conflicting messages and stresses adaptation to local language and farmer context.^15 A translation is not publishable based on literal accuracy alone; it must be checked with intended users for comprehension and unintended risk.

Changes should record the editor, reviewer, source changes, effective date, and affected app content. If a source is withdrawn, expired, superseded, or materially contradicted, dependent advice should be hidden until reviewed.

## 19. Unresolved Philippine research backlog

The following must remain flagged **expert validation required**:

1. Philippine growth-stage-specific action thresholds compatible with WormWise's exact evidence definitions.
2. A nationally endorsed damage-rating scale and mapping to app risk categories.
3. Regional differences in planting calendars, FAW pressure, and reassessment intervals.
4. A reviewed Philippine look-alike photo library with diagnostic annotations.
5. Species-level inventory and identification guide for Philippine natural enemies in maize.
6. Current locally available and registered biological-control products.
7. Current FPA-registered chemical options, labels, IRAC groups, expiry dates, and resistance-management constraints.
8. Validated advice for Bt maize events currently registered in the Philippines, including refuge and resistance-management requirements.
9. Grain and feed disposition guidance after FAW-associated ear damage or mold.
10. Translation review for Filipino and regional languages, ensuring technical terms retain meaning.
11. Accessibility validation for low-literacy users and offline image descriptions.
12. Field validation showing that guide users correctly distinguish current evidence, old damage, and non-FAW injury.

## 20. Release acceptance criteria

An offline article is publishable only when:

- scope, jurisdiction, and evidence grade are present;
- every decision-changing claim is cited;
- at least one qualified reviewer is named;
- no prohibited content appears;
- all photos are licensed, geographically appropriate, and identity-reviewed;
- safety warnings are visible before—not after—hazardous actions;
- terms match the live WormWise assessment fields and computations;
- Filipino translation, if present, has human technical review;
- the article works fully offline;
- stale or time-sensitive content has an automatic expiration rule.
